Insulin Coma Therapy (ICT) was a form of treatment for schizophrenia that emerged in the 1930s and persisted across insane asylums across Europe and North America until 1970. Although psychiatrists expressed concern about the efficacy of the treatment and physicians of other disciplines cautioned about hazardous side effects, the treatment was particularly popular among practitioners for the first few decades of its use. A group of psychiatrists and caretakers remained loyal to the treatment even when alternative shock therapies and antipsychotics emerged, defending its utility against detractors. ICT, as this article will illustrate, demanded more attentiveness from nurses compared to other treatments, and posed severe risks of bodily harm for those subjected to it. These characteristics of ICT would seem to make the treatment unappealing to providers, yet they actually supported its adamant defense by the few who stood by it in the 1950s and onward.
